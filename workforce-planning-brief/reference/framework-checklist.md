# Framework checklist

The full theoretical framework this skill enforces, in one place. Read this before the audit phase (05), or any time it's useful to hold the whole standard in mind at once rather than piecing it together from individual phase instructions.

## The four components (non-negotiable)

- Scope — boundaries: departments/units, roles, geography, timeframe, explicit exclusions.
- Assumptions & Constraints — assumptions are beliefs about the future the plan depends on; constraints are real limitations that don't move.
- Risks & Mitigations — potential events that could derail the plan, each paired with a concrete response.
- Success Criteria — specific, measurable, time-bound metrics tied to business goals.

## The bridge (the mechanism that keeps the plan connected to the business)

Business Strategy → Required Capabilities → Workforce Plan. Every section should trace back to this chain. A plan that reads as generically applicable to any company at any time is a sign the bridge was skipped or ignored.

## Planning horizon

1 year (operational) — tactical, detailed, risks being reactive and missing longer-term shifts.
3 years (tactical) — the usual best balance; a rolling 3-year plan is the standard default unless the case gives a clear reason otherwise.
5 years (strategic) — for major industry disruption or fundamental business-model change; less precise, more assumption-dependent.

Note *why* the chosen horizon fits, briefly, rather than only asserting it.

## The four canonical risk categories

1. Talent scarcity — a critical skill set is harder to find than expected.
2. Budget cuts — funding for the plan is reduced.
3. Low technology adoption — employees resist new tools/workflows, undercutting expected productivity gains.
4. Unexpected attrition — a key person leaves, creating an unplanned gap.

A brief should address all four, even if some get folded into more specific, scenario-grounded language. Silent, unnoticed omission of one of these four is the single most common quality gap this framework is designed to catch.

## Weak vs. strong success criteria

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Improve data skills." | "Increase data-science-certified employees by 40% within 2 years." |
| "Support the product launch." | "Reduce time-to-hire for critical engineering roles by 30%, ensuring the launch is fully staffed by Q3." |

A criterion without a number is a slogan, regardless of how confident or specific-sounding the language around it is.

## The two-move rule: AI drafts, a human personalizes

An AI-generated first pass gives structure and a starting point. A person's job is to review it, fact-check it, and replace placeholders with real organizational data — as a visible, separate move, not something quietly folded into a single generation step. This is, per the source course material, "the most important step" in the whole process — treat it that way, not as an optional polish pass.

## Common mistakes (from the source course material — use as an audit checklist)

- Using generic prompts that don't name the company, the initiative, or specific focus areas — output quality tracks prompt specificity directly.
- Copying AI output without critiquing it — treating a first draft as final rather than as a draft to fact-check and refine.
- Writing success criteria without a number, percentage, or clear binary outcome.
